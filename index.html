Early Virtual Metrology for Plasma Etching

Can machine signals tell us how deeply a wafer will be etched before its process run finishes? I built a model that estimates the final wafer-average silicon etch depth using only sensor readings from the first two minutes of a roughly ten-minute Bosch etch.

The question

Physical wafer measurements arrive after processing. I tested whether in-situ process readings from the start of a wafer's etch could estimate its eventual mean silicon etch depth. The sensor traces record wafer processing after conditioning, so this analysis predicts a wafer outcome rather than the conditioning completion time.

Methodology

01 · SUPERVISED TARGETRegression target: wafer-average etch

I aggregated 89 spatial silicon-etch measurements into one continuous target per wafer, then joined the targets to process traces: 88 labeled wafers across 10 lots.

02 · TEMPORAL FEATURESTime-series feature engineering

I identified the active etch from RF power. For pressure, Gas5 flow, platen Vpp, reflected power, and helium pressure, I calculated window means, standard deviations, and late-minus-early changes at 120 s, 300 s, and the full roughly 598 s.

03 · REGULARIZED MODELRidge regression with L2 regularization

I compared wafer order, sensor features, and their combination using Ridge regression (α = 10). The modeling pipeline applied median imputation and feature scaling before fitting; regularization helps stabilize a linear model with correlated sensor summaries.

04 · GROUPED VALIDATIONLeave-one-lot-out cross-validation

I trained on nine lots and tested on the tenth, repeating this for all 10 lots. Imputation and scaling were fitted within each training fold to prevent preprocessing leakage. I compared out-of-lot predictions using mean absolute error (MAE, µm).

Scope of the result

This analysis estimates the final wafer-average etch depth before the etch finishes. It does not establish conditioning completion, safe early stopping, or saved time and material. Only 10 lots from one dataset were available, and the sensor choices and 120-second horizon were explored using these same lots. A new set of lots is needed to test the selected approach independently.
Data: Sayyed et al., BOSCH plasma-etching dataset. Related work has predicted etch depth from process signals, including Gaussian-process virtual metrology and a full-run wafer-mean benchmark. This project examines prediction using short early sensor windows.

Pit Window Lab: Should Max Pit Now?

At the end of lap 20 in the 2024 Singapore Grand Prix, Max Verstappen was in second place. Stopping would give him fresh tyres, but he would lose time in the pit lane and might return near other cars. I used race data to explore that trade-off.

The moment we replayed

Max was second, 15.156 seconds behind the leader after lap 20. We froze the timing data at that moment, then asked what might happen if he pitted. Later race events were kept out of the decision.

Who might Max come out near?

Each dot below is another driver. The number on the horizontal axis shows how many seconds behind Max that driver was before a possible stop. If Max loses time in the pits, those cars continue moving while he is stopped.

Move the slider to try a different assumption. This time loss has not been measured precisely.

Those cars:

“Near” means close in the timing data. It does not prove that Max would be stuck behind them or tell us how much time traffic would cost.

Would pitting now beat waiting one lap?

Fresh tyres could make the next lap faster. Waiting avoids an immediate stop but means another lap on older tyres. We do not have a reliable measured traffic penalty, so the data cannot give a definite answer. With the example assumptions below, the two choices differ by just 0.05 seconds: too close to call.

Methodology Used

Time-series data and a fair cutoff

I joined laps, tyre stints, pits, race positions, and gaps. The replay uses only records available by the end of lap 20.

Forecasting and model comparison

I predicted a driver's next normal lap from recent laps. A rolling-median baseline was compared with Ridge and Huber regression, tested on later years.

Safety Car data cleaning

Stops during a Safety Car or VSC distort pit timing. Filtering those race periods left only three potentially usable earlier Singapore stops.

Decision sensitivity

I changed the assumed stop time and traffic effects to show when the possible conclusion changes.

How accurate was the pace forecast? The selected rolling-median baseline missed the next normal lap by 0.449 seconds on average in the 2024 test race. Huber regression improved that to 0.430 seconds, a small 0.019-second gain below our preset threshold for adding complexity.

What we learned, and what we cannot claim

In the earlier 2023 Singapore race, the median “extra time across the pit-in and out laps” looked like 96.42 seconds across 24 stops. After excluding Safety Car and VSC periods, only three candidate stops remained: 26.29, 26.84, and 29.45 seconds. That extra-time measure includes more than the stop itself and the sample is small. The 2024 Singapore median of 28.97 seconds is a later check, not information available at lap 20.

Takeaway: We can forecast ordinary lap pace and show which rivals might be nearby under different stop-time assumptions. We cannot prove the best pit lap from these data because the true traffic cost and the result of a pit stop that did not happen are unknown.
Data: OpenF1 API. Race context: 2023 Safety Car, 2023 VSC, and 2024 race context. Historical educational replay, not an official team strategy tool.
